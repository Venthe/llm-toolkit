# Reference: Worlds and Places

These examples describe specific qualities that resonate with the intended approach. They are not templates to copy and are not claims that every work must satisfy every criterion.

## Experiential worlds and strong sense of place

### Morrowind — acquired familiarity

Vvardenfell initially feels alien through its geography, architecture, culture, and soundscape. Repetition turns the strange into the familiar. The silt strider's sound is a particularly effective anchor: unusual at first, then so integrated into the player's mental baseline that its absence would feel wrong.

**Use this lesson:** Let distinctive sensory details become ordinary through repeated exposure. Do not explain away every unfamiliarity.

### Karnaca and Dunwall — cities with different identities

Dunwall is memorable for its decay and oppressive atmosphere. Karnaca, however, feels especially inviting to inhabit imaginatively: warm, coastal, socially textured, with balconies, markets, streets, sea air, neighborhoods, and ordinary residents. It is not simply "the sunny city."

**Use this lesson:** Make cities locally specific. Climate, architecture, sounds, work, public spaces, and ordinary routines should combine into a place with a life beyond its plot.

### Night City — urban life and indifference

Night City feels alive because it does not appear to organize itself around V. Districts differ in architecture, class, advertising, music, food, gangs, and social atmosphere. People argue, work, commute, celebrate, and suffer without needing to involve the protagonist.

**Use this lesson:** Make the setting feel indifferent to the player. Distinct neighborhoods should imply distinct lives, not just different visual palettes.

### Horizon Zero Dawn and Forbidden West — discovery and civilizational memory

The initial descent into the Old World ruins creates wonder and unease before the player understands what happened. Later discoveries deepen the meaning of places already explored. Datapoints and remnants reveal a civilization through ordinary people and institutions, not only famous figures.

The APOLLO storyline gives that continuity emotional weight. Ted Faro's earlier culpability is grounded in recognizable hubris, failed assumptions, and attempts at containment. Destroying APOLLO is a distinct moral break: he deliberately deprives future humanity of its inheritance to satisfy his own delusion.

**Use this lesson:** Let mystery accumulate through environmental evidence. Make the past feel like a real society, and make the loss of its memory consequential.

### Homeworld — civilization through place and loss

Homeworld makes a civilization emotionally tangible through its history, music, ships, migration, and the destruction of Kharak. A restrained line or image can imply an enormous collective experience.

**Use this lesson:** Let large-scale history become personal through specific places, artifacts, and restrained moments. Avoid explaining every emotion.

### Frostpunk — a society shaped by conditions

The Generator, the cold, machinery, scarcity, and collective survival create a strong social identity. The setting's systems and atmosphere reinforce one another, but the world is more than its survival mechanics.

**Use this lesson:** Let environment and infrastructure shape customs, institutions, work, and moral compromises.

### Hardspace: Shipbreaker — the ordinary work of the future

The setting is compelling through the texture of labor: shifts, equipment, corporate structures, routine, risk, and the lonely environment of orbital work. The future is not presented only through exceptional heroes or spectacular events.

**Use this lesson:** Build a world from the perspective of people who work there. Make the routine feel specific.

### Scrapland — a premise with social consequences

A robot civilization after humanity has its own logic, institutions, occupations, and absurdities. The premise generates cultural and social details rather than remaining a one-line gimmick.

**Use this lesson:** Follow a premise into everyday consequences. Ask how society, law, status, work, and identity develop under its assumptions.

### Dishonored — distinct cities

Dunwall and Karnaca are not interchangeable examples of the same setting. Their atmosphere, climate, architecture, social texture, and daily rhythms create different experiences.

**Use this lesson:** When a setting contains multiple cities, make each one locally coherent rather than assigning each a single theme.

### Watch Dogs 2 — life outside the mission

The San Francisco setting is memorable partly through moments such as the beach party: people socializing and enjoying themselves without the plot needing to advance.

**Use this lesson:** Include scenes whose purpose is to let the audience inhabit the world, not deliver objectives or exposition.

### Outer Wilds — a compact, learnable world

A small solar system becomes deeply familiar through exploration. Its locations, music, history, and interconnections form a coherent mental model.

**Use this lesson:** A world does not need enormous scale. A carefully connected small setting can become more inhabitable than a sprawling but generic universe.

### Battlestar Galactica — a society in transit

The Fleet is more than a sequence of battles. It is a remnant of humanity carrying culture, ritual, institutions, grief, and practical problems through space.

**Use this lesson:** When a society is displaced, show the work of preserving ordinary social life as well as the headline conflict.

### Cloudpunk and Dredge — atmosphere at compact scale

Both demonstrate that a relatively constrained setting can leave a strong impression through atmosphere, discovery, and a specific sense of place.

**Use this lesson:** A small world can feel complete without being exhaustively explained. Concentrate detail rather than expanding scale for its own sake.

### Fallout — setting identity

The appeal here is the distinctive world, its history, atmosphere, and lived-in texture. This is separate from reactions to the Fallout television adaptation, whose tonal and lore choices have raised different concerns.

**Use this lesson:** Protect the identity and internal logic that make an established setting recognizable, especially when adapting it.

## Worlds that illustrate the difference between lore and inhabitation

### Star Wars and Warhammer 40,000

Both settings contain enormous lore and provide fertile ground for stories. Yet a planet can be reduced in practice to one location and one broad cultural shorthand, while narratives focus on exceptional characters and conflicts. Vastness and lore density alone do not create a strong sense of ordinary local life.

**Use this lesson:** Do not confuse the number of planets, factions, historical events, or codex entries with experiential depth. Different places should produce meaningfully different everyday lives.

### The Outer Worlds and Hogwarts Legacy

The Outer Worlds did not create the kind of place the audience wanted to explore or inhabit. Hogwarts Legacy offers the appeal of a nostalgic setting, but the world can feel static when routines and rules do not make the school seem convincingly inhabited.

**Use this lesson:** A recognizable premise or beloved IP is not enough. Show the rhythms and systems that make the setting feel active.

## Systems and rules expressed through the world

### Andor — institutional logic

Political and institutional incentives shape events. Organizations have procedures, interests, and limits that affect what individuals can accomplish.

**Use this lesson:** Let institutions constrain characters in concrete ways. Avoid using factions as interchangeable labels for good and evil.

### The Expanse — autonomous factions and interacting systems

Political and material constraints create conflicts among actors with independent interests.

**Use this lesson:** Give groups separate goals and resources. Let their agendas intersect because of the world they share.

### Chernobyl — physical and institutional systems

The story is driven by the interaction of technical reality, institutional incentives, and human decisions.

**Use this lesson:** Let the system's rules and failures drive the story rather than relying on arbitrary villainy.

### The Martian and Gravity — operational constraints

The Martian demonstrates iterative problem-solving under explicit constraints. Gravity offers an engaging operational problem-solving loop, even if some orbital-mechanics details are imperfect.

**Use this lesson:** Technical literalism is not the only goal; the audience must be able to understand and trust the problem-solving model the story establishes.

### Factorio and Kerbal Space Program — agency and feedback

These games demonstrate the satisfaction of direct agency, experimentation, deep systems, and visible feedback.

**Use this lesson:** When systems are central, make cause and effect legible so the audience or player can form and test hypotheses.

## Symbolic, fantastical, and absurdist coherence

### Cloud Atlas — symbolic structure

Symbolic connections and abstract structures can be satisfying when they form a coherent pattern.

### Coco and Pacific Rim — fantastical rules

Fantastical premises work when the story establishes legible rules and meaningful consequences.

### Loki — absurdity with internal logic

An absurd premise does not need realism. It needs to remain consistent with its chosen logic.

**Use this lesson:** Do not equate coherence with realism. Establish the mode, then honor it.


## Additional examples of culture, atmosphere, and character

### Hallownest — identity through fragments

*Hollow Knight* creates a strong sense of place through its visual language, music, environmental storytelling, and remnants of a civilization. The world is communicated through what remains as much as through direct explanation.

**Use this lesson:** Let ruins, artifacts, repeated motifs, and environmental details imply a history that the audience assembles gradually.

### STALKER — life inside a hostile place

The Zone's atmosphere is reinforced by small social moments, especially the campfire songs. These scenes make the setting feel inhabited by people who have developed routines and culture within an extraordinary environment.

**Use this lesson:** Even in dangerous settings, show the social rituals and quiet moments that make life there recognizable.

### Cyberpunk 2077 — culture at street level

Night City is compelling not only as a corporate dystopia but as a lived urban culture. Its music, slang, advertising, architecture, districts, and public spaces work together to make it feel specific.

**Use this lesson:** Avoid making a city only a thesis statement about its politics. Give it pleasures, habits, humor, work, and contradictions.

### Altered Carbon — a society shaped by a changed premise

The setting's treatment of identity and mortality creates consequences beyond the technology itself. A premise becomes culturally significant when it changes institutions, relationships, social status, and ordinary assumptions.

**Use this lesson:** Follow speculative technology or magic into social and personal consequences.

### Star Wars and Warhammer 40,000 — stories versus inhabitable settings

These are settings in which the audience may enjoy individual stories without developing the same attachment to everyday places. A large canon and many named worlds do not guarantee that each location has a distinct ordinary life.

**Use this lesson:** It is valid for a fictional universe to be a compelling story engine without every part of it becoming a place the audience wants to inhabit.

# Reference: Anti-patterns to Avoid

These are recurring risks, not absolute prohibitions. A work can violate one preference and still succeed through strength elsewhere.

## World and setting

### 1. Lore volume mistaken for world depth

**Failure:** Add timelines, factions, wars, names, planets, and encyclopedic history while leaving ordinary life generic.

**Correction:** Show how the setting changes what people do every day. Make local culture experiential, not merely documented.

### 2. Planet-as-biome design

**Failure:** A planet is defined by one biome, one settlement, one visual gimmick, or one culture.

**Correction:** Add regional variation, social differences, infrastructure, history, and local routines. Avoid making every location a themed set.

### 3. Interchangeable locations

**Failure:** Locations differ only by color palette, enemies, or one environmental gimmick.

**Correction:** Differentiate them through climate, architecture, work, food, social structure, customs, sound, and local concerns.

### 4. Protagonist gravity

**Failure:** Every person, event, and location exists to deliver a quest, reveal, or reaction to the protagonist.

**Correction:** Give actors independent goals and ongoing activity. Let the world continue when the protagonist is absent.

### 5. Exposition instead of evidence

**Failure:** Explain a culture or historical event in a block of lore before the audience has experienced anything that makes it matter.

**Correction:** Let architecture, objects, behavior, sound, records, and conflicting perspectives establish the world.

### 6. Every detail is a clue

**Failure:** Each object, line, or location exists to foreshadow a twist or advance the plot.

**Correction:** Include details that are simply part of daily life. Texture is not the same as foreshadowing.

### 7. Explaining away all mystery

**Failure:** Fill every gap, answer every question, and document every corner of the setting.

**Correction:** Leave room for extrapolation. The audience should feel that the world continues beyond what has been shown.

## Narrative structure and causality

### 8. Rule mutation

**Failure:** Established mechanics, constraints, or supernatural rules are ignored or softened when inconvenient.

**Correction:** Keep the model trustworthy. If the rules change, establish why and show the consequences.

### 9. Convenient escalation

**Failure:** New threats, institutions, or resources appear when required, without prior grounding.

**Correction:** Make major developments emerge from existing pressures, actors, and conditions.

### 10. Disposable consequences

**Failure:** Injuries, locations, relationships, or subplots are introduced for impact and then quietly discarded.

**Correction:** Preserve consequences when the narrative has taught the audience they matter. Track changes in resources, knowledge, relationships, and institutions.

### 11. Un-earned resolution

**Failure:** Coincidence, emotional intensity, or happenstance does the work that reasoning and action should do.

**Correction:** Let capabilities, information, resources, and decisions produce the outcome.

### 12. Set-piece-first construction

**Failure:** Spectacle dictates the story and the narrative is assembled around memorable scenes.

**Correction:** Make memorable scenes emerge from character, situation, world rules, and established stakes.

### 13. Disposable episodic storytelling

**Failure:** Episodes are self-contained but lack internal logic, consequence, or satisfying resolution.

**Correction:** Local stories need not alter the wider world, but should still have meaningful stakes and complete causal arcs.

## Character and tone

### 14. Performative characters

**Failure:** Characters constantly announce their cleverness, trauma, menace, uniqueness, or importance.

**Correction:** Let personality emerge from choices, habits, competence, restraint, contradictions, and relationships.

### 15. Erratic characterization

**Failure:** Characters act against their established motivations or knowledge simply to create drama or reach a required plot outcome.

**Correction:** Make surprising behavior understandable in retrospect. Give major transformations a legible psychological path.

### 16. Cartoon motives in serious drama

**Failure:** Institutions or executives pursue catastrophic outcomes for a simplistic, self-declared evil purpose.

**Correction:** Model incentives, constraints, bad assumptions, organizational behavior, and incremental compromise. A deliberate turn toward extreme wrongdoing should reveal a character's beliefs and delusion, not replace characterization.

### 17. Undifferentiated cynicism

**Failure:** Every faction is equally corrupt or ridiculous, leaving little contrast or meaningful moral texture.

**Correction:** Use precise satire. Preserve differences in values, incentives, competence, and behavior.

### 18. Shock without purpose

**Failure:** Cruelty or grotesque content exists mainly to provoke.

**Correction:** Make it serve characterization, stakes, consequences, or the intended tone.

### 19. Forced seriousness

**Failure:** Comedy, absurdity, or power fantasy is treated as though it must justify itself with philosophical depth.

**Correction:** Let entertainment be enough when that is the work's promise. Keep the execution coherent with the chosen mode.

### 20. Universal unlikability

**Failure:** A cast is dominated by characters the audience cannot meaningfully invest in.

**Correction:** Provide contrasts, competencies, relationships, or points of connection. Characters need not be morally good or conventionally likable, but the cast should offer meaningful ways to engage.

## Craft and presentation

### 21. Simulation mistaken for atmosphere

**Failure:** Assume complex AI, economy, physics, or procedural systems automatically make a world feel alive.

**Correction:** Treat simulation and world identity as separate design goals. Use systems to support a place with a strong cultural and sensory identity.

### 22. Generic soundtrack and soundscape

**Failure:** Music and ambient sound provide generic emotional cues without expressing the identity of the place or culture.

**Correction:** Give each setting a sonic vocabulary. Let recurring sounds become familiar through exposure.

### 23. Overwritten lore

**Failure:** Explain the setting in detail when a short scene, object, or interaction could communicate it more vividly.

**Correction:** Prefer demonstration and inference. Keep reference material available for the author or game master, but let the actual story reveal only what is useful in the moment.

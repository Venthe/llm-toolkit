# Reference: Characters, Competence, Progression, and Tone

## 1. Character credibility

Characters should behave in ways that follow from their personalities, circumstances, knowledge, and motivations. They can be cruel, foolish, irrational, hypocritical, absurd, or morally reprehensible. The issue is not whether the audience approves of them; it is whether their behavior feels psychologically and causally credible.

Avoid characters becoming arbitrary instruments for a plot point or theme. A serious story asking the audience to accept a major psychological transformation needs to make that transformation legible. If a story uses loops, alternate timelines, supernatural mechanics, or other unusual structures, those mechanics should have meaningful consequences for character behavior.

A character's behavior should not change merely because the narrative needs a different outcome.

## 2. Character contrasts and contradictions

Strong contrasts between appearance, personality, capability, and behavior can be compelling. Examples include:
- an apparently innocent or harmless person who is exceptionally ruthless when survival demands it;
- a cute or unassuming creature that becomes frighteningly capable;
- a brilliant investigator whose outward affect does not advertise her intelligence;
- a powerful character whose casual behavior makes the scale of their abilities unexpectedly funny.

A contradiction becomes interesting when it has consequences and reveals something about the character. Avoid using contrast as a superficial gimmick.

## 3. Competence: four distinct pleasures

Do not assume competence must always be earned through struggle.

### Earned escalation

Establish a baseline, show learning and adaptation, and eventually deliver an outcome that would once have seemed impossible. The result feels earned because the audience witnessed the process.

**Example: Kumoko in So I'm a Spider, So What?** The appeal is the process of learning the rules, adapting, surviving, and eventually reaching the dragon fight. The human-side plot was less compelling.

### Revealed competence

Withhold the full scale of a character's ability. A seemingly small action can reveal accumulated knowledge or experience that the audience had not fully appreciated.

**Examples:**
- Fern in *Frieren*: progression from a non-mage to an extraordinarily capable mage, with the scale revealed through combat.
- Frieren breaking the barrier: a small action reveals an enormous body of accumulated knowledge.

### Casual overpowerment

A character may already have overwhelming power, or acquire something absurdly valuable with little effort. The disproportion itself can be entertaining. Not every victory needs to be hard-won.

**Example: Rimuru in That Time I Got Reincarnated as a Slime.** The casual acquisition of extremely high-ranking demons can be funny precisely because it is treated almost as an administrative matter.

### Knowledge-based competence

A character notices details, investigates, understands mechanisms, and applies knowledge to solve problems. Their intelligence is demonstrated through situations rather than repeatedly asserted.

**Examples:**
- Maomao in *The Apothecary Diaries*: observation and investigation applied to concrete problems within an unfamiliar society.
- Myne in *Ascendance of a Bookworm*: practical goals draw the protagonist into manufacturing, commerce, institutions, and social structures.

## 4. Retrospective power scaling

A later scene can change how the audience understands what a character has become or what an experienced character was capable of all along. This can be more satisfying than repeatedly announcing a character's level of ability.

Demonstrate accumulated knowledge and capability through action, reaction, contrast, and consequence.

## 5. Progression as a way to expose the world

Give a character a concrete goal, then make achieving it depend on understanding the world. The protagonist discovers that the goal requires materials, skills, people, institutions, resources, relationships, or hidden rules. Each practical problem opens another part of the setting.

This makes worldbuilding active rather than decorative. The audience learns how the world works because the character needs to operate within it.

**Example: Ascendance of a Bookworm.** The appeal includes a progression path where a practical desire for books leads into manufacturing, trade, institutions, and social structures.

## 6. Isekai and outsider perspectives

The isekai label is not the underlying preference. It conveniently creates contrast between a protagonist and an unfamiliar world's baseline. That makes it easy to show how the world works and how the protagonist's knowledge, capabilities, status, or influence compare with it.

The same structure can work without reincarnation or transportation. An outsider entering an unfamiliar society, or a character learning the hidden rules of their own world, can provide a similar dynamic.

The useful question is not "Is this isekai?" but "Is the protagonist's relationship with the world's rules and baseline interesting?"

Examples that illustrate this appeal include *KonoSuba*, *Zero no Tsukaima*, *That Time I Got Reincarnated as a Slime*, *So I'm a Spider, So What?*, and *Ascendance of a Bookworm*.

## 7. Character-specific examples

- **Tanya — The Saga of Tanya the Evil:** contrast between innocent appearance and ruthless competence; pragmatic avoidance of bloodshed until survival demands extreme action.
- **Overlord:** the power and status premise can be entertaining, but Ainz's casual psychological transition from human to accepting extreme cruelty is not sufficiently convincing.
- **Re:Zero:** enjoyable overall, but loop-dependent changes in character behavior can undermine psychological continuity.
- **Higurashi:** the accumulation of loop memories becoming psychologically meaningful is an especially effective use of supernatural mechanics.
- **Maomao — The Apothecary Diaries:** intelligence is demonstrated through noticing, investigating, and applying knowledge.
- **Fern and Frieren — Frieren:** demonstrated competence and accumulated experience can be more effective than explicit declarations of power.

## 8. Tone, darkness, and comedy

The preference is not limited to serious or highbrow stories. Heavy, morally complicated material can coexist with light entertainment and comedy.

Grotesque, disturbing, or morally extreme content is acceptable when it serves the story. Shock value alone is less compelling. Comedy can be a sufficient reason to enjoy a work; it does not need to conceal a deeper philosophical argument.

Power fantasy is a legitimate pleasure. Do not force every protagonist to be vulnerable, balanced, or challenged at all times.

Examples:
- **KonoSuba:** comedy and absurd character interactions are sufficient; the work does not need to justify its entertainment through seriousness.
- **The Mandalorian (early seasons):** low-stakes episodic adventure can be satisfying because the journey itself matters.
- **Tanya the Evil:** tactical competence and a striking appearance/behavior contrast.
- **Loki:** an absurd premise can remain coherent without becoming realistic.

## 9. Adapt to the medium

In games, discovering and inhabiting a world is especially important. The player should feel they are exploring a place with its own rhythms and ordinary life.

In anime, inhabitation is not required. Character dynamics, progression, competence, tactical payoffs, comedy, and memorable moments can matter more. Do not apply the game-world preference as a universal requirement across media.

More generally, identify the experience a work is offering. Do not assume it must be dark, complex, realistic, lore-heavy, or challenging. A work can be an excellent fit because it is simply fun.

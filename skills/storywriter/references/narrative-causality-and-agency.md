# Reference: Narrative Causality, Continuity, and Agency

This file focuses on the structural side of the storywriter approach: stories should respect their own rules, characters should behave as agents, and outcomes should emerge from established conditions.

## 1. Trustworthy rules

The setting can be realistic, fantastical, speculative, absurdist, or symbolic. The audience should be able to understand its operating logic well enough to reason about what could plausibly happen next.

A simplified or technically imperfect model can work. What breaks trust is changing or ignoring the model when a scene needs a convenient outcome.

Apply this principle to:
- physics and engineering constraints;
- technology and magic;
- political and institutional incentives;
- ecology and social arrangements;
- supernatural mechanics;
- symbolic structures and recurring motifs.

A system may remain partly hidden, provided its behavior is inferable and consistent. Reveal it through events and consequences whenever possible.

## 2. Causality, continuity, and persistence

Stories should have memory. Events change the state of the world. Consequences persist when the story has established that they matter. Decisions affect later options, relationships, institutions, resources, knowledge, and risks.

Important developments should emerge from prior conditions rather than disconnected escalation. Resolutions should account for established constraints and information.

This does not require one continuous plot. Episodic adventures can be effective when each episode is a complete and meaningful unit. A local adventure need not alter the wider world, but it should have its own internal logic and satisfying resolution.

**Self-contained is not the same as disposable.**

## 3. Autonomous agents

Institutions, factions, communities, and individuals should have independent goals and ongoing activities. Their paths intersect because they inhabit the same world, not because the author needs a confrontation.

For each major actor, define:
- goals and priorities;
- beliefs and assumptions;
- what they know and do not know;
- resources and constraints;
- allies, rivals, dependencies, and obligations;
- what they are doing if the protagonist does nothing.

The protagonist is not the only source of motion. The world should not wait for them, and major threats should not appear only when the story needs a new escalation.

## 4. Psychological and causal credibility

Characters should act in ways that follow from their personalities, circumstances, knowledge, and motivations. They can be cruel, foolish, irrational, hypocritical, absurd, selfish, or morally reprehensible. Approval is not the measure; credibility is.

A surprising action can be effective if it becomes understandable in retrospect. A major psychological transformation needs a legible path. Character behavior should not change merely because the plot needs a different outcome.

The same applies to protagonists. Arbitrary decisions undermine problem-solving and make consequences feel unearned.

## 5. Competence and problem-solving

Characters should confront problems, understand constraints, evaluate options, and act. Knowledge, preparation, and coordination should create meaningful advantages. Solutions should follow from established capabilities and available resources.

The audience should see problems solved, not merely resolved through coincidence, sudden emotional revelation, or arbitrary intervention. Failure is acceptable when it follows from the situation; success is satisfying when it follows from the character's capabilities and choices.

## 6. Spectacle and set pieces

Spectacle should express the world's rules and constraints, not substitute for them. Action should respect the established physical or fantastical model. Violence should serve characterization, stakes, or consequences rather than existing solely for shock.

Reject a set-piece-first approach in which memorable scenes dictate the plot and the narrative is assembled around them. Memorable scenes should emerge from the situation, character, and world.

## 7. Satire and contrast

Prefer precise satire to indiscriminate exaggeration. If every institution and character is equally absurd or corrupt, contrast disappears. When everything is maximally awful, misery can become normalized and the satire loses force.

Serious institutional or corporate wrongdoing is often more persuasive when it emerges from incentives, competition, hidden risks, denial, cost cutting, and cumulative compromise than from a simple desire to do evil.

A deliberately absurd comic villain can work because the exaggeration is part of the premise. A serious drama needs a credible chain of motives and decisions.

## 8. Symbolism and abstraction

Symbolism, allegory, mythology, and abstract structures are welcome. They need not be mechanically realistic. Their relationships should be consistent enough that the audience can perceive a meaningful pattern.

The question is not whether the story is literal. It is whether its chosen mode has rules and honors them.

## 9. Episodic stories and local stakes

Do not require constant escalation, galaxy-scale stakes, or a single continuous plot. Effective forms can include:
- a western-like journey of local adventures;
- a procedural built around recurring problem-solving;
- a survival story about a small group or isolated community;
- a self-contained episode whose importance is local rather than global.

Low stakes are not meaningless stakes. A small problem can matter when constraints are clear and the outcome has significance within that story.

## 10. Examples and counterexamples

- **Andor:** institutional logic, distributed agency, earned outcomes, and persistent consequences.
- **The Expanse:** autonomous factions, constraints, and interacting political and material systems.
- **Chernobyl:** physical and institutional rules drive the story.
- **The Martian:** iterative problem-solving under explicit constraints.
- **Gravity:** a compelling operational problem-solving loop, despite some orbital-mechanics inaccuracies.
- **Cloud Atlas:** abstract and symbolic connections can work when internally consistent.
- **Coco / Pacific Rim:** fantastical systems can have legible rules and meaningful consequences.
- **Loki:** absurdity can work when the narrative respects its own logic.
- **The Mandalorian (early seasons):** episodic, low-stakes western-like storytelling where the journey itself matters.
- **Breaking Bad:** a counterexample where parts of the world felt protagonist-centered and escalation elements insufficiently grounded.
- **Mr. Robot:** a lower-ranked example despite apparent thematic and technical fit.
- **Fallout (TV series):** concerns about tonal inconsistency, spectacle without consequence, and lore or faction choices that weaken the setting's logic.

These are examples of individual reactions, not universal judgments about quality.


## 11. Character congruence and gravitas

Some characters are compelling because their presentation and behavior feel aligned. They do not constantly perform an identity or explain why they are formidable. Their authority emerges from composure, competence, priorities, speech, restraint, and how other characters respond.

Kreia, Karan S'jet, and Ketheric Thorm are useful reference points. Ketheric's calm authority, performance, and a line such as "Now try again" convey menace without requiring the character to announce it.

**Technique:** Write the behavior and its effects, not the adjective you want the audience to apply. Let the audience infer gravitas.

## 12. Corporate and institutional antagonists

A serious corporate antagonist should not need to announce a desire to destroy billions of people for profit. More credible paths include competitive pressure, cost cutting, hidden risks, organizational denial, distorted incentives, and decisions that appear locally rational while creating systemic danger.

Ted Faro is a useful contrast. His responsibility for the Faro Plague is catastrophic, but initially recognizable as hubris, failed assumptions, and attempts at containment. Destroying APOLLO is a different moral act: he deliberately cripples humanity's best chance to inherit its history and knowledge to satisfy his own delusion.

A deliberately absurd comic villain, such as Dr. Evil, can work because the exaggeration is the premise and the character's behavior remains consistent with it.

**Technique:** Make motives legible at the scale of the character. If a character makes an extreme choice, establish what they believe, what they fear, what they stand to gain, and why alternatives no longer seem acceptable.
